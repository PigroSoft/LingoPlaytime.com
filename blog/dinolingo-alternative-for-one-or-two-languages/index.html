A Dinolingo alternative if your family needs one or two languages, not fifty

28 October 2026 · 4 minute read

Dinolingo is the biggest of the language apps for kids, with 50 languages, ages 2 to 14 and tens of thousands of activities, and it is ad-free. Most families only need one or two languages, and for those families the size and the price are what to weigh.

What Dinolingo says it is

The Dinolingo site calls itself "the best language learning app kids love", says "all 50 languages are included in one family plan", offers videos, songs, games, quizzes and stories for "children ages 2 to 14", counts "40,000+" online activities, and calls the experience "safe and ad-free". It offers a seven-day free trial and then, by its own figures, $19 a month or $199 a year. An Educational App Store review gives it five stars and likes the dinosaur rewards and videos, and it also lists a drawback: the content "sometimes feels unfinished after a few lessons", plus pronunciation inconsistencies such as Cantonese not following the Hong Kong standard. That review quotes slightly different prices, so check the current price before you start a trial.

When fifty languages is more than you need

The range is a real strength for a family that wants to try something unusual, and for a child with one or two target languages most of it goes unused while the monthly fee carries on. A very wide age band also means a toddler and a ten-year-old share one product, and what suits one may not suit the other. Much of the content is video, and a meta-analysis of 59 reports finds children up to six learn about half a standard deviation less from video than face to face, so sitting next to your child matters whichever app you use. Spreading a child across many languages has a cost too: studies of bilingual and trilingual toddlers find that vocabulary in each language follows how much of it the child hears (Place and Hoff, 2011, Côté and colleagues, 2019), which is a reason to choose one or two languages and give them real time. A narrow, well-built activity can teach, as a mobile game built around ten words did for four-year-olds in two studies (Dore and colleagues, 2019).

What LingoPlaytime does instead

LingoPlaytime is sixteen small games with one job. You enter your child's name, your home language and up to four languages you want them to hear, and every tap says a word in one of those languages in a recorded voice, with the name worked in. It is completely free for now, with no ads and no accounts. Good places to start are At the market, Noah's Ark and Pairs. It covers a handful of languages instead of fifty, and it has no subscription for now.

It teaches single words, with no reading, grammar or progress charts, and it is meant to be played next to you so that you say the word too and point at the real thing. It does not replace speaking the language at home, and the plan for that is in how to raise a bilingual child.

Which one to pick

For a family wanting an unusual language or older children who will use a lot of video content, Dinolingo's breadth is worth a trial.
