A Studycat alternative for a toddler who just wants to tap

28 October 2026 · 4 minute read

Studycat makes structured game lessons such as Fun Spanish, aimed at children from about 3 up to the primary years, on a subscription. That works well for a child who can follow a lesson, and a two-year-old who mostly wants to press buttons and hear a word is a different user.

What Studycat says it is

A review hosted on Studycat's own site describes Fun Spanish as "designed by teachers for kids of ages 3-10", a starter program of more than 70 activities covering listening, speaking, spelling, vocabulary and simple sentences, and praises it for teaching Spanish "implicitly" through games. Because Studycat publishes that review itself, it is not independent. The Educational App Store, which is, awards it five stars, praises a natural mix of learning and fun, notes a few activities with odd requests (such as asking a child to put the toilet on the bookshelf), says subscriptions start at $14.99 a month, and mentions a seven-day trial with full access. That page gives an age range of 6 to 13, so the ages differ between sources and are worth checking in the app before you pay.

Where it fits and where it doesn't

Spelling and sentences suit a child who can read a little and follow a lesson, and they ask more than a two-year-old can give. Fun Spanish is also its own app, so a family with a second target language needs another one. Lessons against play is less settled than the marketing suggests: a 2022 meta-analysis of 17 studies with 3,893 children aged 1 to 8 found guided play beat direct instruction for early maths, shape knowledge and task switching, found no difference for most other outcomes, and did not look at second languages (Skene and colleagues, 2022). For the youngest children the useful thing is hearing the same few words often from a voice and a person, and in a study of 88 toddlers, the children who learned a new word from a video were those whose parent joined in.

We should be honest about tapping, too, because the evidence on it is mixed. In a study of 170 children aged 2 to 4, dragging a named object taught some groups more than tapping it, possibly because tapping is an automatic response that takes little thought (Russo-Johnson and colleagues, 2017), and in another study, 24- to 36-month-olds learned a word from touchscreen video only when they had to touch the specific object being named, with the effect depending on age (Kirkorian, Choi and Pempek, 2016). So a tap helps when it is tied to the thing being named, which is how we build ours, and we do not claim that mashing buttons alone teaches a language.

What LingoPlaytime does instead

LingoPlaytime is sixteen small games with one job. You enter your child's name, your home language and up to four languages you want them to hear, and every tap says a word in one of those languages in a recorded voice, with the name worked in. It is completely free for now, with no ads and no accounts. Good places to start are At the market, Noah's Ark and Pairs. Button mashing is the intended way to play at two, and children aged 5 to 7 get asked for a particular word.

It teaches single words, with no reading, grammar or progress charts, and it is meant to be played next to you so that you say the word too and point at the real thing. It does not replace speaking the language at home, and the plan for that is in how to raise a bilingual child.

Which one to pick

For a child of 5 or 6 who likes lessons, Studycat is a reasonable subscription to try, ideally using the free trial first. For a toddler, start with something free that asks nothing of them, and see the language pair guides for your languages. We compared another popular app in our Lingokids piece.
