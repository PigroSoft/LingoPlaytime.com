A Lingokids alternative for learning a second language, not just watching and tapping

28 October 2026 · 5 minute read

Lingokids is a big, well-liked kids' app, and by its own description it is now an entertainment platform with some English in it, which makes it a fine way to fill a quiet half hour and an odd choice for raising a child who speaks a second language. If the second language is the point, a smaller and more focused kind of game does that job better.

What Lingokids says it is

The Lingokids home page is headed "Ultimate kids entertainment", its page title calls it "the #1 entertainment platform for young kids", and the word "language" does not appear in the headline text we could read. The App Store listing counts more than 4,000 games, songs and shows across reading, maths, science, music and art, social skills, history, geography and movement, with 650 or more learning objectives, and lists a Spanish version of the content, though language learning is not named as one of the subject areas. It began differently: in 2017, TechCrunch described it, then called Monkimun, as offering language lessons as interactive games, in partnership with Oxford University Press. There is nothing wrong with an app growing up, but a catalogue built to cover many subjects for ages 2 to 8 is aiming at a different target than a toddler who says manzana for the first time.

What reviewers found

Common Sense Media rates it for ages 3 and up and sums it up as a "big mix of early learning activities" that "can get pricey". It scores educational value as "some", says children can learn plenty of new English vocabulary from the numbers, shapes, colours, food and animals sections, and calls the games "a bit more of a mixed bag". Its main complaint is that there is "lots of content and no guidance for kids in how to navigate through it". It confirms there is no advertising and that access is by subscription. Notice that the vocabulary it credits is English, so for a child whose target language is German or Italian the review does not say much either way.

What it costs

At the time of writing the Lingokids help centre lists $13.49 a month or $161.88 a year on its own website after a seven-day free trial, with different prices through the app stores and in different countries. Common Sense Media calls the price a big downside. A subscription is a fair way to avoid ads, and it is worth knowing before you start a trial that a toddler app you pay for every month still has to earn its place on the tablet.

Why a second language needs something narrower

Toddlers learn words from hearing the same few of them, many times, from a voice that clearly belongs to the language, ideally with a person beside them who says the word too. An open catalogue of thousands of activities works against that, because a child who taps around meets each word once and moves on, and this is our reading of the design rather than something the studies measured. What the studies do show is how much the grown-up matters: in a study of 88 toddlers, children whose parent joined in with a video learned a new word from it and children watching without that lead didn't, and a meta-analysis of 59 reports puts the average learning gap from video at about half a standard deviation for children up to six. A big entertainment app is easy to hand over and walk away from, and that is exactly the use that teaches least.

What LingoPlaytime does instead

LingoPlaytime is sixteen small games with one job. You enter your child's name, your home language and up to four languages you want them to hear, and every tap says a word in one of those languages in a recorded voice, with the name worked in. Words come round again instead of disappearing into a catalogue, button mashing is a welcome way to play at two, and older children get asked for a particular word. There are no ads and no accounts. Good places to start are At the market, Noah's Ark and Pairs.

It also has real limits. It teaches single words, with no reading, maths, grammar or progress charts, and it is meant to be played next to you, so that you say the word as well and point at the real apple. It does not replace speaking the language at home, which is what the research says matters most, and you will find that plan in how to raise a bilingual child and which language you should speak to your child.

Which one to pick

If you want a wide stock of preschool content, songs and shows and you are happy to pay monthly, Lingokids does that and plenty of parents like it. If you want your child to hear words in German, Spanish, French, Italian or Portuguese, start with something built for that, keep the screen time short, and play along. Plenty of families will sensibly use both. For which languages go well together, see the language pair guides.

Questions parents ask

Is Lingokids good for learning a second language?

It started as an English-learning app and Common Sense Media credits it with teaching English vocabulary, but it now describes itself as an entertainment platform with content across many subjects. It is not built around helping a child hear and say words in a second language.

Does Lingokids have ads?

No. Lingokids and Common Sense Media both say it has no advertising. Access is by subscription after a free trial.

Is there a simple alternative to Lingokids for a second language?

LingoPlaytime is a set of small word games in several languages with a recorded voice, no ads and no accounts. It teaches words rather than a full curriculum and works best when a parent plays along.
