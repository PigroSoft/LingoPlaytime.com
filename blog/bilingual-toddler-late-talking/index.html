Is my bilingual toddler a late talker? What the research says

17 October 2026 · 6 minute read

Learning two languages doesn't cause speech delays. Bilingual kids who seem behind are usually further along than they look, because their words are split across two languages and most checklists only count one.

This is one of the most common worries in multilingual families, and it often comes from well-meaning relatives, or even professionals, suggesting you drop a language "until they catch up". The research points the other way.

Why bilingual vocabularies look smaller than they are

Say a toddler hears German and English. She says "Hund" for dog, "ball" for ball, "Milch" for milk and "more" for more. Count only the German and you get two words. Count only the English and you also get two. Count both and you get four, which is her real vocabulary.

Researchers who count this way, across both languages and without double counting words that mean the same thing, find that bilingual children know roughly as many words as monolingual children. The same review says plainly that bilingual kids are not more likely than monolingual kids to have language difficulties.

So when you check a milestone list, add up the words in every language your child speaks. A little notebook on the fridge where both parents jot down new words works well, and it's usually reassuring.

Mixing and switching are normal

Bilingual toddlers borrow shamelessly. "Mehr juice," an Italian verb with an English ending, an answer in the "wrong" language: all expected. Researchers call mixing a normal part of bilingual development and a sign of resourcefulness, since the kid just grabs whatever word they've got. Bilingual adults do the same thing all day.

You don't need to correct it. An easy move is to repeat the sentence in your language so they hear the word they were missing: "More juice? Yes, mehr Saft."

A quiet phase can be normal too

Kids who meet a new language later, say when daycare starts in a language they've never heard, often go quiet in it for a while. They're listening and soaking it in. Many start talking after a few weeks or months, sometimes in whole phrases, which makes for a great surprise at pickup.

When it's worth asking a professional

Bilingualism doesn't cause a delay, but it doesn't protect against one either, so trust your gut. Milestone checklists like the CDC's milestones by age are a useful guide once you count words across all languages. Talk to your pediatrician or a speech and language therapist if, counting every language, your child isn't using any words by around 18 months, isn't putting two words together by around two, doesn't seem to understand simple requests, or seems to be losing words they used to have.

When you do see someone, tell them about every language your child hears and ask for an assessment that counts all of them. Dropping a language is rarely the answer, and it often means a parent stops talking to their child in the language they're most comfortable in.

Meanwhile, keep talking, reading and playing in every language you've got. For ideas on keeping the home language strong, read how to raise a bilingual child.

Questions parents ask

Do bilingual children start talking later?

Research reviews find that bilingual children reach language milestones on a similar timeline when words in all their languages are counted. Their vocabulary in each single language can look smaller.

Should we drop one language if our toddler is talking late?

Usually not. Bilingualism does not cause delays, and dropping a language often means a parent stops using the language they speak most comfortably with their child. Ask a professional for an assessment that counts every language.

Is it bad when my toddler mixes languages?

No. Mixing words from two languages in one sentence is a normal part of bilingual development and shows the child is using every word they know.

When should I worry about my bilingual toddler's speech?

Counting every language, talk to a pediatrician or speech and language therapist if there are no words by around 18 months, no two-word phrases by around two, little understanding of simple requests, or a loss of words.
