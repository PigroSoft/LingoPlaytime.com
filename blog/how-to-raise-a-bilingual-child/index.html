How to raise a bilingual child: what actually works in the toddler years

17 October 2026 · 7 minute read

If you're raising a kid with two or three languages, you've probably collected a pile of rules by now: one parent, one language, only the minority language at home, never mix, always mix. Here's what the research says actually decides it: how much of each language your child hears and uses, and above all whether the language that's quiet outside your front door gets enough room inside it. A method your family can keep up for years beats a perfect one you drop by March.

Good news: there's no secret technique you're missing. The slightly less fun news is that who speaks what at the dinner table will shape your child's languages more than any class, app or flashcard.

Pick a plan you can actually keep up

Most families land on one of a few setups. With one parent, one language, each adult sticks to their own language, so a kid in Berlin might get German from one parent and Italian from the other. With minority language at home, everyone at home speaks the language the neighborhood doesn't, and daycare, friends and the playground handle the local one. Some families split by time or place instead, like Spanish on weekends or English at grandma's.

Researchers who review this work call one parent, one language useful, and also neither necessary nor sufficient for becoming bilingual. A single bilingual parent can raise a bilingual kid, and plenty of families who follow the rule to the letter still watch the minority language fade. What decides it is usually how much of each language a child hears, and whether they ever have to use it.

What nearly 1,900 families told one researcher

The clearest numbers come from a big survey by the linguist Annick De Houwer, who asked 1,899 families in Flanders, the Dutch-speaking part of Belgium, which languages the parents used at home and which ones their 6 to 10 year olds actually spoke. Every child spoke Dutch, since that's what the community speaks. Far fewer spoke the family's other language, and the setup at home made a big difference.

When both parents spoke only the minority language at home, almost every child spoke it too. When each parent stuck to a different language, about three in four did. When one parent used both languages and the other used only Dutch, it dropped to about a third. François Grosjean's summary of the study walks through each group, and the original paper concludes that one parent, one language was neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for success.

A survey can't prove cause and effect, and families who go all in on the minority language probably differ in other ways too. Still, the direction is hard to ignore: the community language shows up on its own, and the home language needs a bodyguard.

How much is enough?

Nobody can hand you an exact number, but the research gives a sense of scale. Byers-Heinlein and Lew-Williams note that a child generally needs at least 10 to 25 percent of their exposure in a language to learn to speak it, and that both quantity and quality count. Quality means live conversation with real people, where an adult answers what the child says, names what they're looking at and takes turns. A TV droning in the background does very little.

To check your own balance, walk through a normal week and estimate the hours your child spends in each language. If daycare is nine hours a day in the local language and the home language gets breakfast and bedtime, the local language is going to win, and that's your cue to add more.

Make the home language the fun one

Kids learn the words that come attached to things they care about. If the home language is the language of songs, silly voices, bath time and picture books, a toddler has a reason to want it. If it's only the language of "put your shoes on," you can see why they'd prefer the other one.

Mixing languages is normal

Expect your kid to mix: a German word in an English sentence, an Italian question answered in German. Researchers call this a normal part of bilingual development, a sign the child is using every word they have. More on that in our post on bilingual toddlers and late talking.

Questions parents ask

Will learning two languages confuse my toddler?

No. Research reviews find that babies tell their languages apart from early on and show no signs of confusion. Mixing words from both languages is a normal part of bilingual development.

Is one parent, one language the best method?

It works for many families, but studies describe it as neither necessary nor sufficient. In a survey of 1,899 families, children were most likely to speak the minority language when both parents used it at home.

How much exposure does a child need to learn a language?

There is no exact figure, but researchers suggest a child generally needs at least 10 to 25 percent of their language exposure in a language to learn to speak it, ideally through live conversation.

Is it too late to start a second language at three?

No. Young children keep picking up languages throughout the preschool years. Starting later simply means planning for enough regular, playful exposure.
